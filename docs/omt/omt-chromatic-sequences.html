半音模進

Bryn Hughes

重點整理

  • 自然音模進在同一調內，依規律移調並重複音樂片段。
  • 經半音化的自然音模進，可以加入半音裝飾或半音和弦，例如副屬和弦；但它不會嚴格保持移調音程的度數與性質兩者都相同。
  • 半音模進與自然音模進不同：整段模進都保持相同的移調音程度數與性質。自然音模進則保持度數，卻容許性質改變，以便留在同一個調內。
  • 無論哪一種模進，各聲部的連接方式都必須前後一致；每次反覆中相互對應的和弦，其聲部配置也應相符。
譯註：這裡「音程度數」與「音程性質」分開看，例如大二度與小二度都是二度，卻不是相同距離。真正的半音模進要求每次原型移調的度數與性質一致，不是看到升降記號就成立。

KEY TAKEAWAYS

  • Diatonic sequences repeat musical segments and are transposed in a regular pattern within a key.
  • Chromaticized diatonic sequences can include chromatic embellishments or chromatic chords, such as applied (secondary) dominants. These sequences avoid strict transposition of both interval size and quality.
  • Chromatic sequences differ from diatonic sequences in that both the size and quality of the interval of transposition is maintained throughout the sequence. Diatonic sequences preserve the interval size, but not the quality, to ensure that they stay within a single key.
  • Remember, with all sequences, the voice leading must be consistent within every voice. Chord voicings should match between all corresponding components.

下行五度模進

先看下面常稱為「下行五度模進」的例1。

自然音下行五度模進譜例

靜態樂譜尚待補齊；原始資源含有本章必要的音樂範例。

例1。自然音下行五度模進。

這個模進原型包含一次根音下行五度的進行；後續每次重複，都把整個原型下移二度。由於所有和弦都來自 G 大調，根音移動的音程不會從頭到尾完全相同。例如，IV 與 vii° 之間的根音移動是增四度，其餘相鄰和弦之間則是完全五度或完全四度。我們用這種稍作調整的方式，讓音樂留在同一個調。如果相鄰根音之間始終保持完全五度或完全四度，根音就會依序走 G–C–F–B♭–E♭–A♭–D♭……。模進很快便會離開 G 大調，進入新的半音化領域，成為半音模進。
半音模進與自然音模進有幾個重要差異：

  • 模進原型與後續各次重複的起始和弦，會涉及變化音級。
  • 每次重複中，相互對應的和弦都維持相同的性質與類型。
  • 這些模進與等分八度的進行有關。

要注意，半音模進並不只是「含有半音變化音的模進」。例2仍是同一個下行五度模進，只是隔一個和弦就加入副屬和弦。它雖然包含副屬這類半音和弦，卻不算真正的半音模進，因為整體走向仍沿著單一調的音階級移動。請留意，連續各小節第一拍的和弦根音，仍與例1的純自然音版本相同：G–F♯–E–D。

加入副屬七和弦的自然音下行五度模進

靜態樂譜尚待補齊；原始資源含有本章必要的音樂範例。

例2。隔一個和弦加入副屬七和弦的自然音下行五度模進。

反過來，只要確保根音進行從頭到尾都保持一致的音程模式，就能做出真正的半音模進。一個簡單方法，是把模進原型的第二個和弦改成屬七和弦，使它成為下一次原型起始和弦的屬七。在例3中，原型的第二個和弦改成 F7，不再是自然音 IV 和弦。我們將它解讀為下一個和弦的 V7，而接下來的和弦又是另一個屬七和弦。

譯註：前兩例的文字以 G 大調說明，但此段改寫第二和弦為 F7，並稱原先是自然音 IV；若仍在 G 大調，IV 的根音應為 C。這表示此處不能把 F7 直接當成前述 G 大調 IV 的同根音改寫。例3可能另換了起始調，仍須取得原譜核對；譯文保留原文 F7 與 IV，不猜改音名。
屬七和弦連鎖的半音下行五度模進

靜態樂譜尚待補齊；原始資源含有本章必要的音樂範例。

例3。副屬七和弦環環相接的半音下行五度模進。

上例的聲部連接需要特別留意。每個和弦都被解讀為下一個和弦的屬七，因此不可能同時讓導音與和弦七音都按慣例解決。連接根音相距五度的七和弦時，聲部連接會涉及省略式解決：原本預期接在屬七和弦後的和弦，被另一個同根音的屬七和弦取代。例如，G7 之後原本預期出現 C 大三和弦或 C 小三和弦；省略式解決卻改接 C7，見例4。譜中以括號標出原本預期的 C 和弦。這種解決實際上將預期和弦與後續的 C7 合併省略，彷彿在聽覺上跳過預期和弦，直接前往下一個屬七。結果是：第一個屬七和弦的導音 B，向下半音解決，成為新和弦的七音；同時，原和弦的七音 F 向下半音解決，成為新的導音。這種導音與七音的角色交換，是上例這類七和弦連鎖進行的重要聲部連接方式。它也是爵士和聲的重要基礎，本書其他部分還會談到。

譯註：本段所說的省略，具體指屬七接另一個屬七，例如 G7→C7：B→B♭ 成為新七音，F→E 成為新導音。不是任何兩個根音相距五度的七和弦，都必須把導音降低；要依和弦性質與功能判斷。

回到例3，請注意每個連續強拍上的根音，以大二度將八度等分。這使調性變得模糊，羅馬數字分析也難以穩固成立；尤其譜中以星號標出的和弦，只是為了分析標記的一致性，才如此標示。分析高度半音化的音樂時，常常很難為和弦指定羅馬數字，而這種調性模糊本來就是此類音樂的美感之一。遇到這種情況，搭配使用和弦代號（lead-sheet symbols）往往更方便，本章譜例也提供了這些代號。

Descending-Fifths Sequence

Consider the following example (Example 1), often referred to as the "descending-fifths sequence."

Musical example

Static score pending; the original resource contains a musical example.

Example 1. A diatonic descending-fifths sequence.

The sequence model, a root progression by descending fifth, is transposed down by second in each subsequent copy of the model. Because the sequence uses chords entirely from the key of G major, the root motion doesn't match exactly throughout the sequence. For example, the root motion between the IV and viio chords is an augmented fourth, whereas the root motion between every other pair of chords is either a perfect fifth or perfect fourth. We "cheat" in the sequence in this way in order to keep the music within a single key. If the interval between successive chord roots was consistently a perfect fifth/fourth, the root progression would be as follows: G–C–F–B♭–E♭–A♭–D♭… and so on. The sequence would rather quickly bring the music outside of the key of G major, and into new chromatic territory. It would become a chromatic sequence.
Chromatic sequences differ from their diatonic counterparts in a few important ways:

  • The chords that initiate the sequence model and each successive copy contain altered scale degrees.
  • The chords within the pattern are of the same quality and type as those within each successive copy of that pattern.
  • The sequences derive from those that divide the octave equally.

Importantly, chromatic sequences are not merely sequences that contain chromatic pitches. Example 2 shows the same descending-fifths sequence, this time with alternating secondary dominant chords. While the sequence contains chromatic chords (the secondary dominants), it is not a truly chromatic sequence because the overall trajectory of the sequence is still one that traverses the scale steps of a single key. Notice that the progression of chord roots on successive downbeats still matches the purely diatonic sequence shown in Example 1: G–F♯–E–D.

Example 2. A diatonic descending-fifths sequence with alternating secondary dominant-seventh chords.

Conversely, we can create a truly chromatic sequence if we ensure that the progression of chord roots maintains a consistent pattern of intervals throughout the sequence. An easy way to do this is to make the second chord of the sequence model into a dominant-seventh chord that can be applied to the first chord of the subsequent copy of the model. In Example 3, the second chord of the model is now F7 instead of a diatonic IV chord. We interpret this as V7 of the chord that follows, which is, in turn, another dominant-seventh chord.

Example 3. A chromatic descending-fifths sequence with interlocking secondary dominant-seventh chords.

The voice leading in the above sequence requires some attention. Because every chord is interpreted as a dominant-seventh of the chord that follows, it is not possible to resolve both the leading tone and the chordal seventh as normal. As is the case whenever you connect seventh chords with roots a fifth apart, the voice leading requires an elided resolution. Instead of the chord you expect to hear following a dominant-seventh chord, you get a dominant-seventh chord with the same chord root. For example, we expect to hear either a C or Cm chord following a G7 chord. An elided resolution would result in a C7 chord in place of the expected chord. An example of an elided resolution is shown in Example 4. The example shows the expected C resolution in parentheses. The elided resolution essentially "elides" the chord we expect with the following chord, C7. In a sense, we mentally skip over the expected chord to get to the next dominant-seventh chord. An important result of the elision is that the leading tone of the first dominant-seventh chord, B, resolves down by half step to become the new chordal seventh. Likewise, when the chordal seventh in the first dominant-seventh chord, F, resolves down by half step, it becomes the new leading tone. This leading tone/chordal seventh exchange is essential for proper voice leading in chord progressions that use interlocking seventh chords, such as the sequence above. Furthermore, this kind of voice leading is integral to the study of jazz harmony, as you will find in other parts of this textbook.

Example 4. An elided resolution of a dominant-seventh chord.

Returning to Example 3, notice that the progression of chord roots on each successive strong beat divides the octave equally into major seconds. This results in a sense of tonal ambiguity, making the Roman numeral analysis of these chords tenuous, at best. In particular, the chords identified with asterisks in the example are only labeled as such for consistency. In many cases, when analyzing highly chromatic music, it is often quite difficult to assign Roman numerals to chords; this tonal ambiguity is part of the aesthetic of this kind of music. In cases like this, it is often convenient to also analyze the music using lead-sheet symbols. These have been included in the examples in this chapter.

Example 6. A chromaticized diatonic ascending 5–6 sequence, featuring secondary dominant chords.

The above examples present the diatonic ascending 5–6 sequence (Example 5) and its chromaticized variant (Example 6). Note that both of these include an inconsistent pattern of intervals between chord roots in the second measure. To that point, the pattern of chord roots was a descending minor third followed by an ascending perfect fourth. From beat 1 to beat 2 in m. 2, the chord roots are D to B♭—a major third. To make this a truly chromatic sequence, this interval must be corrected to match the others. Thus, we would change the B♭7 to a B7. Likewise, we would then change the chord that follows the B7 to a chord with a root of E (rather than E♭), to preserve the root progression by perfect fourth (Example 7).

Example 7. A chromatic ascending step sequence, featuring secondary dominant chords.

A similar problem arises with the chord qualities used at the beginning of each subsequent copy of the sequence model. The first chord of the sequence is major, so for it to be a chromatic sequence, we must change the remaining first chords of each iteration to be major as well. The final result is a sequence in which the chord on every strong beat is a major triad with roots a major second apart. If it were to traverse the entire octave, the sequence would divide the octave into major seconds. In Example 7, though, the sequence stops once it reaches the E major triad, treats that triad as a dominant chord, and modulates into A major. The modulation brings the music down a half step from its starting key. Distant modulations such as these are one of the reasons that chromatic sequences can be powerful tools.

There are, of course, myriad ways in which we may vary a chromatic sequence. The examples listed throughout this chapter merely represent some of these options. Importantly, all of these chromatic sequences transport the listener away from the tonal center, for the purposes of either a temporary disruption of stability, or to modulate into a distantly-related key. These techniques were used by composers throughout the nineteenth century, and were part of what contributed to pushing tonality to its fringes as we moved closer to the turn of the twentieth century.
